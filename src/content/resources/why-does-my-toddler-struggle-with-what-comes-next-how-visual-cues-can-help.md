##### “Put your shoes on.”

Your toddler keeps playing.

“We need to leave.”

Still playing.

“Shoes. Please.”

Nothing.

By the fourth reminder, you are frustrated.

Your toddler is frustrated.

And somehow a simple instruction has turned into a battle.

It is easy to assume the problem is listening.

Sometimes it is.

But sometimes the problem is much simpler:

**The routine is clear to you because it is happening inside your head. Your toddler cannot see it.**

You know that after breakfast comes getting dressed.

After getting dressed comes brushing teeth.

Then shoes.

Then the bag.

Then you leave the house.

Your toddler may only know:

**I am playing right now, and suddenly Mum wants me to stop.**

That is where visual cues can help.

Pictures, simple routine cards and short visual sequences can make an invisible plan visible.

Instead of depending entirely on repeated verbal instructions, your toddler gets another way to understand:

**What am I doing now?**

**What happens next?**

**When am I finished?**

## Why “What Comes Next?” Can Be Hard for Toddlers

Adults have years of experience with routines.

When you wake up, you probably do several things automatically.

Maybe you use the bathroom, shower, dress, prepare breakfast, find your things and leave.

Nobody needs to stand beside you holding cards that explain each step.

But those routines became automatic through repetition.

Your toddler is still learning them.

Young children benefit from predictable routines because knowing what is coming can make everyday life easier to understand. The CDC notes that daily schedules can help take some of the surprise out of what happens next.¹

ZERO TO THREE similarly explains that predictable routines can help toddlers know what to expect and prepare for changes during the day.²

That does not mean children need every minute scheduled.

It means familiar sequences can give them useful information.

## Spoken Instructions Disappear Quickly

Imagine telling your toddler:

“Finish breakfast, take your cup to the kitchen, go and get dressed, brush your teeth, find your shoes and bring your bag.”

By the time you reach “bring your bag,” the beginning of the instruction may already be gone.

Even shorter instructions can become difficult when a child is:

- deeply involved in play
- tired
- excited
- upset
- distracted
- learning a new routine
- being asked to stop something enjoyable

A picture remains there.

That is one of the useful differences between a verbal instruction and a visual cue.

You can say:

“Shoes next.”

And point to the picture of the shoes.

The child does not need to remember the entire sentence.

The information is still visible.

## What Is a Visual Cue?

A visual cue is simply something your child can **see** that communicates information.

It could be:

A photograph.

A simple illustration.

A picture card.

A two-step First → Then board.

A Now → Next → Later sequence.

A To Do → Done board.

A visual daily schedule.

It does not have to be complicated.

If you photograph your toddler's toothbrush and show that picture before brushing teeth, that is a visual cue.

If you draw a bed beside a picture of a book to show **Book → Bed**, that is a visual sequence.

If you place three routine cards showing **Breakfast → Clothes → Shoes**, that is a visual routine.

The tool matters less than whether your child understands what the visual means.

## Visuals Do Not Replace Your Voice

A common misunderstanding is that using visual cues means you stop talking to your child.

No.

The visual supports what you are saying.

You might say:

“Breakfast is finished. What comes next?”

Then point to the card showing clothes.

“Get dressed.”

Once your toddler is dressed:

“Done. What comes next?”

Then show the toothbrush.

You are still communicating.

But now your words are supported by something your child can see.

Over time, familiar pictures may need less explanation because your toddler begins recognising the sequence.

## Start With the Problem, Not the Pinterest Board

Before creating a giant visual schedule, identify the actual problem.

Do mornings go badly?

Is bedtime chaotic?

Does your toddler struggle when screen time ends?

Do they resist cleaning up?

Does leaving the house take forever?

Choose **one repeated difficulty**.

For example:

Your toddler plays after breakfast and resists getting dressed every morning.

You do not necessarily need a twelve-step daily schedule.

Try:

**Breakfast → Get dressed → Shoes → Leave**

Or even:

**FIRST: Get dressed**
**THEN: Play for five more minutes**

Start with enough information to solve the problem you actually have.

You can expand later if necessary.

## Use Pictures That Are Easy to Understand

Beautiful is not the same as useful.

A routine card can match your home décor perfectly and still be confusing to a toddler.

Choose images that clearly represent the action.

For example:

A toothbrush for **Brush Teeth**

A shirt for **Get Dressed**

Shoes for **Shoes On**

A plate or bowl for **Breakfast**

A bathtub for **Bath**

A book for **Story**

A bed for **Bedtime**

If your child does not recognise a picture immediately, teach it.

Show the card.

Name it.

Do the activity.

Repeat.

The purpose is not decoration.

The purpose is communication.

## Put Visuals Where the Routine Happens

A visual schedule hidden in a drawer cannot do much.

Place it where your child can use it.

A morning routine might be near the bedroom or dressing area.

A handwashing sequence might be near the sink.

A bedtime routine might be in the bedroom.

A leaving-home routine might be near the door.

The CDC recommends placing visual reminders where children and caregivers can easily see them and notes that pictures or visual cues can be useful for toddlers and preschoolers who cannot yet read.³

Consider your child's height too.

If possible, put the visuals somewhere they can comfortably see and interact with them.

## Try First → Then When the Full Routine Is Too Much

Some children do not need to see six steps.

They need two.

**First → Then** answers a very simple question:

“What do I need to do now, and what happens immediately after?”

Examples:

**FIRST:** Put toys away
**THEN:** Bath

**FIRST:** Brush teeth
**THEN:** Story

**FIRST:** Shoes on
**THEN:** Outside

**FIRST:** Get dressed
**THEN:** Breakfast

This format can be especially useful when your child is resisting one specific part of a routine.

And “Then” does not need to be a reward.

You do not have to promise sweets, toys or screen time after every normal task.

Often “Then” is simply the next normal event.

**First pajamas. Then story.**

Both were going to happen anyway.

You are simply showing the order.

## Use Now → Next → Later for Children Who Need a Little More Information

Sometimes two steps are not enough.

Your toddler may keep asking:

“Are we going to Grandma's house?”

“Now?”

“Are we going now?”

Instead of repeating the day's explanation several times, try:

**NOW:** Breakfast
**NEXT:** Get dressed
**LATER:** Grandma's house

Your toddler may not understand “We are leaving at 10:30.”

But they can begin understanding:

Grandma comes **after** these things.

This can make waiting more concrete.

## Let Your Toddler See Progress With To Do → Done

Some children enjoy physically completing a routine.

You can divide a board into:

**TO DO**

and

**DONE**

At the beginning of the routine, the cards might be:

Get dressed.

Brush teeth.

Put shoes on.

When your child finishes getting dressed, move the card to DONE.

Then brush teeth.

Move it.

Then shoes.

Move it.

The child can see the number of remaining tasks becoming smaller.

Instead of Mum being the only person announcing what is left, the board gives some of that information too.

It can also give your toddler a small role in managing the routine:

“You brushed your teeth. Can you move the card to Done?”

That participation can support the independence you are already trying to build.

## Use Visuals Before the Difficult Moment

Do not wait until your toddler is already crying before introducing the routine.

If leaving the playground is difficult, remind your child before leaving.

If bedtime is difficult, review the bedtime sequence before they become exhausted.

If mornings are rushed, show the morning sequence when the routine begins.

ZERO TO THREE recommends helping young children anticipate transitions and using familiar routines, warnings and rituals to make changes easier to manage.⁴

Preparation is usually more useful than trying to explain an entire system during a meltdown.

## Pair Visual Cues With Simple Language

When you introduce the visual, keep your language short.

Instead of:

“I already told you that after dinner you're supposed to go upstairs because it's bath time and then we have to get ready for bed…”

try:

“Dinner is finished.”

Point.

“Next: bath.”

Or:

“First shoes.”

Point.

“Then outside.”

The visual does not need a speech.

The more complicated the moment becomes, the more useful simple language can be.

## What If My Toddler Ignores the Picture?

A visual card is not magic.

You cannot put a toothbrush picture on the wall and expect your toddler to immediately understand the entire concept.

Teach the system.

At first:

1. Show the card.
2. Name the activity.
3. Complete the activity together.
4. Mark or move the card when finished.
5. Show the next step.

Repeat.

Eventually, the picture may start carrying meaning by itself because the child has experienced the same connection repeatedly.

If a visual is consistently ignored, ask:

Is the picture understandable?

Are there too many cards?

Is the routine too complicated?

Is the board somewhere the child can actually see?

Have I taught them how to use it?

Am I using it consistently?

Sometimes the solution is not adding more visuals.

It is making the system simpler.

## Don't Turn the Visual Schedule Into Another Source of Pressure

Your visual routine is supposed to support family life.

It is not supposed to become another standard you feel guilty about failing.

Some mornings will change.

Someone will wake up late.

There will be appointments.

Your toddler will get sick.

Grandma will visit.

Plans will change.

That is life.

A flexible visual system can change too.

Remove a card.

Rearrange the order.

Explain:

“Today is different.”

Then show what will happen instead.

Routines work because they provide a familiar framework—not because every day must be identical.

## Visual Cues Can Support Independence Too

One reason I like visual routines is that they can gradually shift part of the routine away from constant adult prompting.

Instead of:

“What's next?”

“Brush your teeth.”

“Now shoes.”

“Come back.”

“Get your bag.”

you may eventually be able to ask:

“What does your board say is next?”

Your toddler looks.

Shoes.

They may still need help.

They may still need reminders.

But now they have another source of information besides your voice.

ZERO TO THREE gives the example of using visual schedules with toddlers so they can begin understanding ideas such as “I am playing now, but next I will eat lunch,” helping them prepare for what happens next.⁵

That is the real goal.

Not perfect independence.

**Growing independence.**

## Visuals Are Tools, Not Punishments

Avoid turning routine cards into threats.

“You didn't move your card!”

“Look at your board! Why aren't you listening?”

“I'm taking this away because you didn't follow it!”

The board should communicate.

It should not become another thing your child fears getting wrong.

If your toddler struggles, help them.

“Let's check what's next.”

Point.

“Shoes.”

“Would you like to put them on, or do you need help?”

That preserves the purpose of the visual: helping the child understand and participate.

## Start With One Routine Today

You do not need to redesign your child's entire day.

Choose one point where you find yourself repeating the same instruction again and again.

Maybe it is getting dressed.

Maybe it is cleaning up.

Maybe it is leaving the house.

Maybe it is bath time.

Maybe it is bedtime.

Then ask:

**What does my toddler need to understand here?**

Start with two or three simple pictures.

Show them.

Use them.

Repeat the routine.

Adjust what does not work.

Your toddler is not expected to know your schedule simply because you know it.

Sometimes the missing piece is not another reminder.

Sometimes your child simply needs to **see what comes next.**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What age can toddlers start using visual cues?

There is no single age at which every child is ready. Simple pictures can be introduced when your child is beginning to recognise familiar objects, activities and everyday sequences. Younger toddlers may need only one or two pictures, while older toddlers may manage a longer routine.

### Do visual schedules help toddlers who cannot read?

Yes. Visual schedules are designed to communicate primarily through pictures or symbols, so independent reading is not required. Simple written labels can still be included alongside the picture.

### How many pictures should I use?

Start small. If your child struggles with a long sequence, use two steps such as First → Then. Add more information only when it is useful.

### Should I use photographs or illustrations?

Either can work. The most important thing is that your child can understand what the image represents. Some children may respond particularly well to photographs of their own objects or routines, while clear illustrations may work perfectly for others.

### What if our routine changes every day?

Focus on repeated parts of the day rather than trying to schedule everything. You might still have a consistent bedtime sequence even if daytime activities change. Movable visual cards can also be rearranged when plans change.

### Do visual cues stop toddler tantrums?

No tool can guarantee that a toddler will never become upset. Visual cues are intended to make routines and transitions clearer and more predictable. A child may understand exactly what is happening next and still be disappointed.
