You bought the potty.

Maybe you have picked out underwear, saved a few potty-training videos and decided that this is finally the week you are going to start.

But there is one question worth answering first:

**Is your toddler actually ready for potty learning?**

Age alone does not tell you that.

Potty learning asks your toddler to notice what is happening inside their body, understand what that feeling means, stop what they are doing, get to the potty, manage clothing and communicate when they need help.

That is a lot of small skills working together.

Before you start, look at what your toddler is already showing you.

## 1. Your Toddler Stays Dry for Longer Periods

Start noticing how frequently your toddler's diaper is wet.

If they sometimes remain dry for longer stretches, it may be a sign that bladder control is developing.

You are not looking for perfection.

You are simply looking for a change from constant wetting toward longer dry periods.

## 2. They Notice a Wet or Dirty Diaper

Does your toddler tell you after they have pooped?

Do they pull at a wet diaper?

Do they ask to be changed?

This growing awareness matters because potty learning begins with noticing what the body is doing.

## 3. They Show Interest in the Potty or Toilet

Some toddlers follow their parents into the bathroom.

Others want to flush the toilet, sit on the potty fully clothed or ask questions about what you are doing.

You do not have to manufacture this curiosity.

Simply notice whether your toddler is beginning to pay attention naturally.

## 4. They Can Follow Simple Instructions

Using the potty is not one action.

It is a sequence.

Your toddler may eventually need to:

- stop what they are doing
- walk to the potty
- pull clothing down
- sit
- finish
- clean up with help
- pull clothing back up
- wash their hands

They do not need to do all of this independently before you begin.

But understanding simple instructions such as “come here,” “sit down” or “pull your trousers down” can make learning easier.

## 5. They Can Communicate Basic Needs

Your toddler does not need perfect speech.

They simply need a way to communicate.

That may be words such as:

“Potty.”

“Pee.”

“Poop.”

Or it might be a gesture, sign or another signal that you both understand.

The goal is to build a simple shared language around toileting.

## 6. They Are Becoming More Independent

You may notice your toddler trying to:

- pull clothing up or down
- wash their hands
- copy what adults do
- complete familiar routines
- insist, “I do it!”

Those small attempts at independence can support potty learning because your toddler is beginning to participate actively in everyday routines.

## 7. They Can Sit on the Potty Without Strong Fear

Your toddler does not have to love the potty immediately.

But if every mention of it causes intense fear or distress, slowing down may be more useful than pushing harder.

The first goal does not always have to be successfully using the potty.

Sometimes the first goal is simply:

**See the potty → sit on it → understand what it is for.**

That still counts as progress.

## Potty Readiness Is Not All or Nothing

Your toddler may show several signs strongly while still developing others.

That does not automatically mean “ready” or “not ready.”

Instead of asking:

**“Is my toddler old enough?”**

Try asking:

**“What potty-learning skills is my toddler already showing, and where do they still need support?”**

That gives you far more useful information.
