A visual routine works best when it answers one small question: what happens next?

You do not need to picture the whole day on the first attempt. Choose one part of the day that repeats often and already has a familiar order. Getting ready for bed, washing hands or putting shoes on can be a good place to begin.

## Start with three steps

Pick three steps your child already sees you do. Put the pictures in the order they happen. Point to the first picture before you begin and return to the pictures when it is time to move on.

Keep your words short. You might say, “First pyjamas. Then teeth. Then story.”

## Put the routine where it happens

A routine is easier to use when it is visible in the place where the task begins. A bathroom routine belongs near the bathroom. A leaving routine belongs near the shoes or the door.

If a printed page is not practical, keep the same three pictures on your phone and show them before the routine begins.

## Practise when nobody is rushing

Look at the pictures once during a quiet moment. Point to each step and let your child show you what comes next. The aim is familiarity, not a test.

## Change what does not fit

If a picture is confusing, replace it. If five steps are too many, use three. If your family does the routine in a different order, the tool should change to match your home.

A visual routine is a prompt. It does not guarantee cooperation, prevent every hard feeling or replace professional support when a child needs it.
